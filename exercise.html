Exercise: Keeping FMD SAT1 out of Thailand

Work in groups of four or five. Open each task tab in turn and fill in the worksheet for every measure you propose.

Your role

Your role. You are a veterinary officer of the Department of Livestock Development (DLD) in a provincial or regional team. SAT1 has not been detected in Thailand. The Director-General asks your team for a plan in three parts: prevent introduction, monitoring and surveillance plan for early detection, and control an outbreak if one happens.

Task 1. Prevent SAT1 from being introduced

Situation. SAT1/III is in China and Mongolia and moving along Asian livestock trade routes. Thailand has land borders with Myanmar, Lao PDR, Cambodia and Malaysia, large informal cross-border trade in cattle and buffalo, and international airports and seaports.

Think about:

  1. Border and trade measures. Inspection, quarantine and testing of imports, import conditions by commodity and by the exporting country's status (Terrestrial Code Chapter 8.8: for example, for live cattle, residence in a free country or zone for at least 3 months and no FMD within 10 km, or 30 days of isolation with negative tests), bans on products from affected countries, and penalties.

  2. Internal measures. Live-animal markets, transporters, traders, slaughterhouses and farms. Consider market rest days and cleaning, no mixing of consignments, a ban on feeding untreated food waste, quarantine of new animals for 5 to 14 days, visitor and vehicle control, and farmer awareness.

  3. Vaccination. Should Thailand vaccinate against SAT1 before any case? If so, who and where: a buffer zone along high-risk borders, dairy herds, all ruminants? Which vaccine, given r1 values and limited supply? How does it fit with the current vaccination program in Thailand?

  4. Regional action. What would you ask of neighbours, FAO, WOAH and the reference laboratories?

Feasibility and socioeconomic prompts

  • Do officers have the legal power and staff to control informal crossings and thousands of small markets?

  • Who loses income when markets close or imports stop? Does a ban on animal products push trade into illegal channels and increase the risk?

  • What would preventive vaccination cost per animal per year (vaccine, two doses, labour, cold chain, post-vaccination monitoring), and who would pay?

  • Would vaccination affect trade or later surveillance?

Task 2. Surveillance for early detection

Situation. Design a surveillance system for FMD SAT1 in Thailand. It should detect an introduction early and show whether SAT1 is present or absent. FMD types O and A are already endemic, so many vesicular cases will be FMD but not SAT1. The system must be able to identify SAT1 among them.

Think about:

  1. Passive surveillance. How do you get farmers, private veterinarians, para-veterinarians, market staff and slaughterhouse inspectors to report suspicious signs quickly? Consider a hotline, feedback, training, and what happens to a farmer after reporting.

  2. Active, risk-based surveillance. Where would you look first: border districts, live-animal markets, slaughterhouses, dairy areas, pig farms, transport routes? In which species? Which sampling frame, sample type (swabs, vesicular epithelium, bulk-tank milk for dairy herds, serum) and frequency?

  3. Syndromic and early-warning signals. Besides clinical reports, FAO suggests livestock mortality, slaughterhouse observations, milk-yield drops and information from traders and social media. Which would you monitor in Thailand, and who collects them?

  4. Case definition and trigger. Write a suspect-case definition. Which vesicular cases must be sent for serotyping or sequencing, not only for pan-serotype PCR? What actions start in the first 24 to 48 hours?

  5. Laboratory chain. From sample collection to result: transport, biosafety, turnaround time, serotype confirmation, sequencing, and referral to a reference laboratory. Where is the bottleneck?

  6. Serology. In a country with endemic O and A and widespread vaccination, what can structural-protein serology and NSP ELISA tell you about SAT1, and what can they not?

  7. Evidence of freedom. What combination of evidence would you present to a trading partner: passive reports with timely testing, targeted active surveillance, border controls, and documentation of no SAT1 detection? What does "absence of evidence" mean here?

  8. Reporting. What do you report, to whom (DLD, WOAH, FAO, neighbours) and how fast?

Task 3. Control and eradicate SAT1 after an outbreak

Situation (hypothetical). Laboratory testing confirms SAT1 on a dairy farm in a major dairy-producing area. Within days, neighbouring dairy farms report similar signs. The farms range from smallholders with a few cows to larger commercial herds. They deliver milk to cooperative collection centres and share artificial insemination technicians, veterinarians, feed trucks and calf and cull-cow traders. On the index farm the oldest lesions are about 4 days old, cull cows were sold at a live-animal market 8 days ago, and milk is collected daily.

Think about:

  1. Epidemiological investigation. Using the incubation period (2 to 14 days) and the fact that virus is shed in milk up to 4 days before signs, define the window during which you must trace animals, people, vehicles, milk and products forward and backward from the index herd. Which contacts are highest priority (for example milk tankers, AI technicians, feed trucks, veterinarians, and calf and cull-cow traders)?

  2. First 72 hours. List your actions in order: sampling and confirmation, notification to WOAH, quarantine of the farm, a standstill on animal movement, tracing, and setting zones. What distance would you use for the infected, protection and surveillance zones? (The EU, for example, applies at least 3 km and 10 km for the protection and surveillance zones.) What would you adapt for Thailand?

  3. Strategy. Compare stamping out alone, stamping out with emergency vaccination to live, emergency vaccination to cull, and vaccination without slaughter. Use the Terrestrial Code (Chapter 8.8) to find how each choice affects the waiting period for regaining status and for trade, and decide what is relevant for an endemic country.

  4. Vaccine decision. Which vaccine: SAT1 monovalent, from which producer, how quickly can it arrive? How would you check that it matches the field virus (r1, heterologous neutralising titre) and that its potency is adequate (high-potency, at least 6 PD50)? Do you vaccinate ring-wise, the whole province, or high-risk species? How soon after vaccination can you expect protection, and what do you do in the gap? How would you separate vaccinated from infected animals (NSP ELISA, purified vaccine)? What about the O and A programme?

  5. Movement, markets and biosecurity. Which markets, transport routes and slaughterhouses close, for how long, and how do you clean and disinfect vehicles and premises? How do you keep milk collection running safely, and what do you do with milk from infected and in-contact herds?

  6. Species differences. What changes for lactating cows, dry cows, heifers and calves (myocarditis risk), and for goats, sheep and pigs kept on or near dairy farms?

  7. Disposal and welfare. How do you dispose of carcasses, milk, manure and bedding safely, and cull humanely if needed?

  8. Communication. What do you tell farmers, cooperatives, traders, consumers and the media? FMD does not infect people. How do you prevent unnecessary panic about meat and milk?

  9. Ending the outbreak. What evidence shows SAT1 is eradicated, and what surveillance continues?

Feasibility and socioeconomic prompts

  • Can the province find enough trained staff, protective equipment, disinfectant, vehicles and laboratory capacity within days?

  • If animals are culled, what compensation is paid, how fast and from which budget? How does compensation level affect willingness to report (moral hazard and under-reporting)?

  • Who is hit hardest by a standstill and market closure: a smallholder with one cow, a cooperative, a trader, a slaughterhouse worker? What happens to milk collection and the cooperative's cash flow?

  • What happens to exports and national trade if SAT1 is confirmed?

  • Compare costs: an early, strong response versus a slow one that lets the disease reach other provinces. Which data would you need for a partial budget or benefit-cost analysis?
