FMD SAT1: Introduction

FMD SAT1 began spreading out of sub-Saharan Africa in 2023 and reached China and Mongolia by spring 2026. Read the introduction, then go to the exercise.

1. Virology and antigenic diversity

Foot-and-mouth disease virus (FMDV) is a non-enveloped, positive-sense, single-stranded RNA virus of the genus Aphthovirus, family Picornaviridae. The genome is about 8.3 kb. The icosahedral capsid contains 60 copies each of four structural proteins, VP1 to VP4. VP1, VP2 and VP3 are exposed on the surface and carry the neutralising epitopes. VP4 lies inside the capsid and stabilises it.

  • Seven serotypes, no cross-protection: O, A, C, Asia1, SAT1, SAT2 and SAT3. Within a serotype, strains can also differ antigenically, so a vaccine may protect poorly against a heterologous strain of the same serotype.

  • SAT1 (Southern African Territories 1) has 13 topotypes (I to XIII), defined by VP1 sequence. It is maintained in East and Southern Africa with the African buffalo as reservoir, and is the most genetically diverse of the SAT types. SAT virions are generally described as less stable than O and A virions, which makes SAT vaccine antigen harder to manufacture.

Two SAT1 topotypes are now circulating outside Africa.

Topotype Detected in What is known
SAT1/I Qatar (2023); Bahrain, Iraq, Kuwait, Türkiye, Egypt (2025) Closest relatives are East African viruses (Tanzania, Kenya). Qatar 2023 and Bahrain/Iraq 2025 sequences differ by more than 15% in VP1, which supports separate introductions.
SAT1/III Iran, Türkiye, Cyprus, Greece; China and Mongolia (2026) Iran and Türkiye sequences are more than 99% identical to the vaccine strain BOT/1/77. WRLFMD lists vaccine-strain inactivation failure or a plant biocontainment breach only as open questions, not findings.

2. Hosts, pathogenesis and clinical disease

FMDV infects domestic and wild cloven-hoofed animals. It is not a zoonosis. The African buffalo (Syncerus caffer) is the only wildlife species with a significant epidemiological role, and it maintains the SAT types.

Course of infection

  • Incubation: 2 to 14 days. The WOAH Terrestrial Code uses 14 days.

  • Entry and spread in the body: inhalation or ingestion, primary replication in the pharyngeal region, then viraemia and spread to stratified squamous epithelium. Vesicles form in the tongue, dental pad, lips, nares, interdigital skin, coronary band and teats.

  • Excretion: virus is shed in exhaled air, saliva, faeces, urine, milk and semen. Milk and semen can carry virus up to 4 days before clinical signs, so animals are infectious before they look sick.

  • Indirect spread: contaminated hands, footwear, clothing, vehicles, equipment, milk, semen for artificial insemination, and swill containing infected meat. Under some weather conditions, aerosols can travel up to about 60 km over land and 300 km over sea (WOAH).

Species differ in their role in an outbreak

Species Role Clinical picture
Cattle, domestic buffalo Indicator: very susceptible to inhaled virus, signs are usually obvious Fever, anorexia, shivering, a sharp drop in milk yield, then drooling, lameness and vesicles on the mouth, feet and teats. Recovery takes 8 to 15 days.
Sheep, goats Maintenance: often mild or subclinical, so infection can go unnoticed Fever, mild lameness, small oral lesions, agalactia. Lambs and kids may die without signs.
Pigs Amplifier: excrete far more airborne virus than ruminants Fever, severe foot lesions and lameness (worse on concrete), snout vesicles, high piglet mortality.
African buffalo Reservoir of SAT types Usually subclinical; long-term carriers.

  • Mortality: adults 1 to 5%. Young calves, lambs and piglets 20% or more, mostly from myocarditis ("tiger heart").

  • Morbidity in a susceptible herd is close to 100%.

  • SAT1 in goats (experimental): all five goats inoculated intradermolingually with mixed SAT1 isolates (10^4.57 TCID50) had fever within 48 hours, lasting 5 days on average. In-contact goats became febrile on days 5 and 9, and lesions appeared on days 4 and 8 after exposure.

The carrier state. A carrier is an animal that still has virus in the pharynx after the acute phase, without overt disease. In cattle, 15 to 50% become carriers; the state usually lasts under 6 months but can reach 3 years in a few animals. Sheep, goats and domestic buffalo carry for a few months. African buffalo can carry SAT viruses for at least 5 years. Carrier-to-susceptible transmission is rare and hard to prove, but modelling in buffalo suggests carriers account for about one third of potential transmission events and can keep SAT1 going in small herds of fewer than 100 animals. Whether carriers matter for SAT1 in Asian livestock is not known.

3. Distribution and spread

Before 2023. SAT1 was endemic in sub-Saharan Africa, with transmission maintained in East and Southern Africa. Outside Africa it appeared only as short, self-limiting incursions: in West Asia in the 1960s (Iraq, Lebanon, Israel, Jordan, Iran, Türkiye, Kuwait) and in Yemen in 1984.

2023 onward. The Swine Health Information Center (SHIC) briefing describes a shift from sporadic detection to sustained circulation, and concludes that the spread is a real expansion, not an artefact of better reporting.

  • 2023: Qatar (July); Jordan also reported.

  • 2025: Bahrain and Iraq (January), Kuwait (April), Türkiye (May), Egypt (mid-year), Azerbaijan and Iran (October), Lebanon (November).

  • 2026: Cyprus (December 2025 to February 2026, depending on source), Israel and Greece (March), China (March to April) and Mongolia (May).

  • Southern Africa: a SAT1 upsurge cost Botswana and Lesotho their FMD-free status (WRLFMD, 2026).

Sources differ by a month or two for several countries (Iraq, Egypt, Cyprus), and some reports were not confirmed by national authorities. When you cite a date, cite the source.

4. Effects

SAT1 affects animal health, production, income and trade.

Direct losses (at farm level)

  • Milk yield falls sharply and may not return to the previous level. FAO reports significant impact on the dairy sector in West Asia.

  • Weight gain slows, fertility drops, and draught animals lose working capacity because of lameness.

  • Young animals die of myocarditis, so losses extend into next year's replacement stock.

  • Culling, if used, removes the farm's productive assets.

Indirect losses (beyond the farm)

  • Cost of the response: surveillance, vaccine, culling, compensation, disinfection, staff.

  • Trade: loss of market access, or restriction to heat-treated or processed products. Control costs and trade restrictions fall along the whole value chain.

  • Disrupted markets: movement standstills and market closures stop sales when households need cash.

  • Wider food-security effects in smallholder-dominated systems.

Scale.

  • A FAO rapid assessment estimates risk-adjusted direct loss exposure of about USD 2.0 billion a year across 33 Asia-Pacific countries in a medium-impact scenario. It excludes trade disruption, market shocks, loss of herd asset value and response costs, so total impact would be higher.

  • For comparison, FMD in sub-Saharan Africa is estimated to cost more than USD 2.3 billion a year in livestock production losses.

  • Smaller livestock-dependent economies carry a higher relative burden.

Economics to keep in mind in the exercise. Losses and costs fall on different people (private losses for farmers, public costs for government), compensation changes farmers' incentives to report, and prevention has the characteristics of a public good because one farm's biosecurity protects its neighbours.

5. How SAT1 became a threat to Thailand

SAT1 reached East Asia in spring 2026, and Thailand's livestock have no immunity to it. Five facts explain the threat.

1. It is now on the Asian mainland.

  • Xinjiang: first detected on 28 March 2026 at a livestock market in Ili Kazakh Prefecture, about 85 km from the Kazakhstan border. 142 cases, with 513 other susceptible animals on site.

  • Gansu: reported on 2 April 2026 from a farm in Wuwei Prefecture, about 300 km from the Mongolian border. 77 cases, with 5,715 other susceptible animals on site.

  • All reported cases were domestic cattle. China notified WOAH on 16 April 2026. Mongolia followed in May. WRLFMD reports the viruses as SAT1/III.

  • The two sites are about 2,000 km apart. The source of introduction is not confirmed. China's ministry suspects entry across the northwest border. The distance between sites raises the possibility of several introductions or undetected spread.

  • China's O, A and Asia1 vaccines give no protection. China culled and disinfected at both sites, increased border patrols against smuggling, and gave emergency approval to two SAT1 vaccines.

  • On 17 June 2026 FAO put South and Southeast Asia on high alert.

Department of Livestock Development infographic, from WOAH and FMD in China reports, April 2026.

2. Thai animals are immunologically naive to SAT1. FMD is endemic in Thailand with serotypes O and A. The most widespread lineage in the region is O/ME-SA/Ind-2001e, followed by A/ASIA/Sea-97 and O/ME-SA/PanAsia-2. Asia1 has not been reported in Thailand since 1998. Vaccines and herd immunity are built on these types, and none protects against SAT1.

Global FMD status. Red = endemic; mainland Southeast Asia, including Thailand, is endemic for FMD. Source: WRLFMD, Pirbright Institute, via MSD Veterinary Manual (2026).

3. Cross-border movement is already a documented driver of FMD in the region. Space-time cluster analysis in Southeast Asia found that most clusters stay within a country, but some span borders, which supports cross-border transmission. Authors recommend bilateral movement agreements, real-time information sharing, border community engagement, and better market biosecurity and traceability.

4. Value chains are open to a new virus. FAO notes that animals in the region often pass from small farms to traders and live-animal markets before slaughter or border crossing, so virus can move before an outbreak is confirmed. Smallholders often keep large and small ruminants and pigs near the home, and biosecurity varies widely. FAO lists the SAT introduction pathways as livestock movement, legal and informal trade in animal products, wildlife, people, fodder and vehicles.

5. Our tools are matched to old serotypes. Few SAT1 vaccines are available worldwide, and matching of SAT1 field viruses to existing vaccine strains gave variable results. At the Pak Chong reference laboratory, in-house vaccine matching covers O, A and Asia1 only, although PCR, antigen ELISA, isolation and sequencing follow WRLFMD protocols and can detect and type SAT1.
